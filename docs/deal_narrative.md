# The Deal: What Happened, Year by Year

*(All figures cited in `sources.md`.)*

## Before 2014: a declining segment inside a public company

Red Lobster was one of several chains under Darden Restaurants (public,
NYSE: DRI). Darden's own investor presentation shows the segment's EBITDA
declining for several years before the sale: **$350M (FY2011) → $334M
(FY2012) → $307M (FY2013) → $229M (the twelve months ending April 2014)**.
Same-restaurant sales trailed the industry benchmark almost every month for
two straight years. This was not a thriving business — Darden was selling
a chain it had already identified as a drag on its portfolio.

## 2014: the sale that engineered the collapse a decade later

In May 2014, Darden sold Red Lobster to **Golden Gate Capital** for **$2.113
billion** — about 9x that declining LTM EBITDA. Darden's board had explored
a spin-off instead, under pressure from activist investor Starboard Value,
but concluded the Golden Gate sale maximized value and reduced Darden's own
leverage and earnings volatility.

**The mechanism that matters**: Golden Gate didn't actually have $2.1
billion. It paired with real estate buyer American Realty Capital
Properties from the start of the sale process. The result: **$1.5 billion
of the purchase price came from selling Red Lobster's own restaurant real
estate** and signing long-term leases to rent it back. **Golden Gate's own
equity check was only ~$600 million** — covering less than a third of the
deal. This is a more extreme version of the real estate carve-out that
also appears in the Toys "R" Us story, but here it funded the *majority* of
the purchase price, not just a side pocket.

Darden used the proceeds as a normal corporate divestiture would: ~$1
billion to pay down its own debt, and ~$600 million (part of a ~$700
million program) toward share buybacks. Darden shareholders did well out
of this deal. The question this project is really about is what the
structure did to Red Lobster itself.

## 2016: a strategic partner buys in

Thai Union Group, a Thai seafood company, invested **$575 million** for a
**49% stake** (25% common outright, 24% convertible preferred). At the
time, Red Lobster's disclosed trailing financials were **$2.479 billion
revenue** and **$144 million operating profit** — a 5.8% margin, thinner
than the 9-14% EBITDA margins Darden had reported just two years earlier
(not a precisely comparable measure, but directionally consistent with a
business still working through the aftermath of its 2014 restructuring).
The implied equity valuation, ~$920 million, was well below what Golden
Gate had paid for the whole company in 2014 — though that's comparing an
equity value against an earlier enterprise value, so it isn't a clean
value-destruction signal on its own.

## 2020: the sponsor leaves

**Golden Gate Capital fully exited** in 2020, selling its remaining stake
to Thai Union, former Red Lobster management, and a group called "Seafood
Alliance." **This is the pivot point of the whole story.** Whatever
happened to Red Lobster from here on didn't happen on Golden Gate's
balance sheet. The sponsor who engineered the sale-leaseback structure was
gone four years before the structure's cost came due.

What Golden Gate actually made or lost on this exit is not disclosed in
any source reviewed for this project — and this repo does not guess.

## 2021-2022: refinancing, quietly

In January 2021, the company (now owned by Thai Union/Seafood
Alliance/management) arranged a new **$264.7 million Term Loan** with
Fortress Credit Corp as administrative agent. This is the debt that would
still be outstanding — and that Fortress would eventually use to take over
the company — three years later. No further annual financial detail for
this period was found in sources reviewed; this is the stated gap in the
data (see `sources.md`).

## 2023: the collapse

The first-day bankruptcy declaration lays out the mechanics in detail.
Guest counts had fallen **~30% since 2019**. Net sales actually *rose* 25%
from 2021 to 2023 as pandemic-era dining recovered — but then declined
sharply in the twelve months before the filing, during which **adjusted
EBITDA fell more than 60%**. The year closed with a **$76 million net
loss**.

Three things compounded at once:

1. **The inherited lease burden.** $190.5 million in 2023 lease
   obligations — more than $64 million of it tied specifically to
   underperforming stores the company couldn't exit cheaply. This is the
   2014 sale-leaseback's bill, arriving nine years later under completely
   different ownership.
2. **A specific, named operational misstep.** In May 2023, then-CEO Paul
   Kenny made the "Ultimate Endless Shrimp" promotion — historically a
   limited-time event — a **permanent $20 menu item**, over internal
   management objections. The bankruptcy declaration states this decision
   alone cost the company **$11 million**.
3. **Macro pressure.** Restaurant wage inflation outpaced restaurants'
   ability to raise prices without losing more guests, squeezing margins
   industry-wide.

The cash math: **$100 million** of cash in May 2023 became **less than
$30 million** about six months later, driven by roughly $31M of operating
cash losses, a forced $27M paydown of the asset-based credit line
(triggered by a vendor-financing change that shrank the company's
borrowing base), and $32M of interest payments across the year.

## May 2024: Chapter 11

Red Lobster filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on **May 19, 2024**,
in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Middle District of Florida (Orlando
Division). At filing, funded debt was a modest **$294 million** — the
Fortress-led Term Loan plus outstanding letters of credit under an asset-
based facility. On paper, that doesn't look like an over-leveraged
company. It's the $190.5M/year of rent — never classified as "debt" on any
balance sheet — that actually broke it.

## September 2024: the outcome

Fortress Investment Group — the same firm that was already Red Lobster's
senior secured lender — **credit-bid $376 million** (paying with debt it
was already owed, not new cash) and took ownership. The sale closed
September 16, 2024. The reorganized company is owned by Fortress-managed
funds alongside TCW Private Credit and Blue Torch Capital.

**Thai Union, Seafood Alliance, and former management — the owners who
had been holding equity since 2020 — were wiped out entirely.** General
unsecured creditors received interests in a litigation trust rather than
cash.

## What this project argues

The conventional LBO story (Toys "R" Us: one sponsor loads up debt and
rides it to bankruptcy) isn't what happened here. Instead:

- A sponsor extracted real estate value through a sale-leaseback,
  structurally underfunding its own equity check by design (71% of the
  purchase price came from monetizing the target's own assets).
- That sponsor left cleanly, years before the bill came due.
- The structural burden it created — not visible as "debt" anywhere on a
  balance sheet — sat there until a specific operational misstep and a
  traffic downturn combined to make it unpayable.
- The equity that was actually destroyed belonged to the *next* owners,
  who inherited a capital structure they didn't design.
- Ultimately the senior lender, not a new sponsor, ended up owning the
  business — a "loan-to-own" outcome rather than a traditional exit.
